Interoperability without bridges, without custodians

The history of cross-chain value is, mostly, a history of lost coins. Bridges have been the single richest target in the industry precisely because of how they work: you hand your coin to a custodian on one side, and a claim to it appears on the other. The bridge is not a road between sovereign territories. It is a vault you are asked to trust, holding the real asset while you carry around an IOU.

The bridge is a custodian in disguise

Strip away the diagrams and most bridges reduce to the same shape: lock the asset with an operator, mint a representation elsewhere, and hope the operator stays honest, solvent, and unhacked. That is custody. Interoperability built this way does not extend your sovereignty across chains — it suspends your sovereignty for the duration of the trip, and sometimes forever.

If moving between chains means handing your coin to someone who promises to give it back, you did not cross a bridge. You made a deposit.

Continuum's standard

Interoperability should widen what is possible without ever asking you to surrender your keys — and what crosses between chains need not be a coin. It can be a deed, a title, or the two sides of an escrow settling against each other. A cross-chain design earns the name only if it preserves ownership, and agreement, end to end:

  • No party takes custody of the asset in transit — not a multisig, not a federation, not a "decentralised" operator set.
  • The owner can always recover what is theirs unilaterally if the path stalls or an operator misbehaves.
  • What arrives on the far chain is ownership, not a claim against a vault that can default.
  • An agreement that spans two chains still binds only its two parties — no bridge operator is quietly written in as a third.
  • Each chain keeps its own rules; neither is forced to defer to the other to make the transfer work.

Honouring the chains you connect

Real interoperability treats each chain as a sovereign order, connected as an equal. It does not quietly promote one chain to the arbiter of the others, and it does not dissolve their differences into a single machine. Continuum connects chains the way treaties connect nations — cooperation without conquest — rather than the way an empire connects provinces.

Why this is the hard version

Custodial bridges are easy because trust is a shortcut, and shortcuts ship fast. Removing the custodian is harder: it demands cryptographic settlement, unilateral exits, and honest accounting of who secures what. But easy is how the coins were lost. Continuum's wager is that the only interoperability worth building is the kind where, after the value has moved, you still hold exactly what you held before — your keys, your assets, your agreements intact, on your terms.
